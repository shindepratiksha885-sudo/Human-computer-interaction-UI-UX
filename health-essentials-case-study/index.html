Case note · 001

The broken experience

A third-year hostel student sprained her ankle playing badminton after dinner. Nothing dramatic — until the pain set in and she needed a crepe bandage and pain-relief spray immediately. What should have taken minutes turned into a scramble across rooms, roommates, and group chats, while she sat in pain waiting for someone, anyone, to reply.

That gap — between needing a health essential and actually getting it — is the broken experience this file investigates.

Phase 1 · Empathy & Definition

Who is actually waiting on that bandage?

Research started with an assumption, then went and tested it against three real hostel students through short interviews and a supporting survey.

Before research

Proto-Persona

Dipti Sharma, 2nd-year undergrad

Lives in a university hostel. Assumed to always want to stay prepared for minor health emergencies and to recover quickly without interrupting daily life.

Assumed goal

Find immediate help during unexpected health situations and get back to normal fast.

Assumed pain

Doesn't always have health essentials on hand; finding help can be stressful and slow.

After 3 interviews + survey

True User Persona

20-year-old undergrad, university hostel resident

Occasionally faces minor health emergencies — fever, injuries, menstrual discomfort. She doesn't just want medicine. She wants the right essential, fast, so she can get back to her day without wasting time in pain.

Real pain point

Asks roommate first, then friends, then seniors, then the hostel WhatsApp group. Replies aren't instant — the search itself becomes stressful, slow, and uncertain while she's already unwell.

Supporting evidence · hostel survey, 8 respondents

“No body is active on WhatsApp for such things. And I had to reach out to people when I was sick.”— Ayush, on asking for Azithromycin

Core pain point

Even after knowing exactly what she needed, she still had to go room to room and contact several people to find it. Instead of focusing on getting relief, she spent her time searching — while dealing with pain, discomfort, and growing frustration.

Turning the pain point into requirements

Three user stories

01

As a hostel student who has sprained my ankle,

I want to quickly know where I can get a crepe bandage and a pain relief spray,

so that I can treat my injury without spending time searching while in pain.

02

As a hostel student dealing with an insect bite late at night,

I want to easily let nearby students know that I need a specific health essential,

so that I can receive help quickly and focus on recovering.

03

As a first-year hostel student experiencing continuous coughing,

I want to know if a cough syrup is available nearby before my condition gets worse,

so that I can get timely relief and avoid unnecessary stress.

Phase 2 · Ideate & Evaluate

20 features, sorted by 10 real students

Pattern found

Five clear clusters emerged even without pre-set categories: Find Essentials, Requests, Contacts, Medical Support, and My Essentials. Four cards split closer to 40% across two categories — Hostel & Room Number, Emergency Contacts, Update Availability, and Item/Confirm Availability — flagging them as candidates the tree test would need to confirm.

From card sort to structure

V1 Information Architecture → Tree Test → Final IA

The majority clusters became a 5-branch menu tree. Five students then ran a tree test against it, cold — five findability tasks, no visual design, just labels.

How the IA was fixed

Every student expected Request History to live with the rest of their requests, and Campus Emergency Contacts to live with the rest of their contacts — the opposite of where the card-sort majority had first placed them. The Final IA (V2) swaps both: Request History moves into Requests, Campus Emergency Contacts moves into Contacts. Everything else in V1 tested cleanly and was kept as is.

Phase 3 · Prioritization

Budget for an MVP: 4 must-haves, no more

Every feature from the Final IA was sorted with MoSCoW. Only four could be Must-Have — so the two features fought over hardest went through a DFV Matrix to settle it with evidence, not opinion.

Must Have

  • Search Health Essentials
  • Emergency Request
  • Respond to Request
  • Student Contact Details

Should Have

  • Nearby Essentials
  • My Available Health Essentials
  • Add Health Essential
  • Update My Essentials
  • Request Notifications
  • Hostel & Room Number

Could Have

  • Common Health Essentials
  • Available Items
  • Community Help Requests
  • Request History
  • Remove Health Essential

Won't Have

  • Infirmary Contact Information
  • Basic First-Aid Guidance
  • Medicine Information
  • Campus Emergency Contacts

The fight for the 4th Must-Have spot

Two features were debated hardest for the final Must-Have slot: Search Health Essentials and Emergency Request. Both were scored 1–5 on Desirability, Feasibility, and Viability.

Search Health Essentials

15/15
Desirability 5/5
Nearly every interviewee said the first thing they wanted was a quick way to check whether someone already had the item — before asking anyone or waiting for replies.
Feasibility 5/5
Mainly needs a searchable list of health essentials shared by students — straightforward to build for an MVP.
Viability 5/5
The starting point of the whole experience. If users can't search, every other feature becomes less useful.

Emergency Request

13/15
Desirability 4/5
Valuable in urgent situations, but most students said they'd want to check availability first, before creating a request.
Feasibility 4/5
Needs more moving parts than search — creating requests, notifying students, managing responses.
Viability 5/5
Matters when searching alone isn't enough — makes the community genuinely helpful to each other.

Search Health Essentials wins the final Must-Have spot, 15/15 to 13/15 — it's the step every other feature depends on. Emergency Request stays a strong Must-Have on its own merit and remains in scope.
